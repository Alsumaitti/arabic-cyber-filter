Full-Corpus LLM Evaluation to Build a High-Precision Tiered Filtering System for Arabic Cybersecurity Text

Submitted by: Osamah Alsumaitti  ·  Project: Arabic cybersecurity corpus filter for FineWeb2 Request: API budget for full-corpus LLM judging — maximum USD 150

1. Executive Summary

We built a lexicon-based filter that extracted 13,345 cybersecurity documents from one million Arabic web records, then measured its quality scientifically with an LLM-as-Judge evaluation. The measurement exposed two specific weaknesses, and for each we have already developed a working, methodical fix:

  1. A recall gap: the Arabic lexicon was missing the English terms that pervade Arabic technical writing (VPN, Firewall, …) — we mined 24 missing keywords from the missed records themselves and built a unified lexicon of 1,233 keywords.
  2. A precision gap: the filter trusts all keywords equally — we designed a keyword tier system that learns each keyword's individual reliability from the LLM verdicts.

Both fixes are currently built on a 300-record judged sample. The single remaining step to turn them into a production-grade filtering system is judging the corpus in full, at a cost of ~USD 85–120 (via the Batches API at a 50% discount). That budget is the subject of this proposal.

2. Current Status: How We Got Here

Stage 1 — Building the filter (complete)

  • Filtered 1,000,000 Arabic records from FineWeb2 across 100 parallel SLURM tasks.
  • Lexicon of 1,209 terms from the Arabic Cybersecurity Glossary (the output of an earlier stage of this project).
  • Whole-word matching with a full evidence trail: every kept record carries the exact keywords that matched it.
  • Output: a corpus of 13,345 documents, every classification human-verifiable.

Stage 2 — Scientific measurement (complete)

3. Idea 1: Mining the Missing Keywords (complete)

Instead of guessing what the lexicon lacks, we mined the very records the filter missed (17,216 rejected records carrying a broad cybersecurity signal): extract cybersecurity n-grams, deduplicate against the lexicon under proper Arabic normalization, rank by document frequency, then hand-curate.

Result: 24 verified-new keywords, with a structural discovery: the single biggest gap is English terms. Cybersecurity vocabulary is coined in English and enters Arabic writing untranslated — an Arabic author writes “VPN”, not الشبكة الافتراضية الخاصة. The original lexicon was built as an Arabic terminology list, so Latin-script tokens fell outside its scope — a scope gap, not a conceptual one.

Measured impact: the 24 keywords alone recover 916 additional cybersecurity documents the filter was rejecting (VPN by itself appears in 604 of them). Ready deliverable: a unified lexicon of 1,233 keywords, machine-verified free of duplicates.

4. Idea 2: The Tiered Keyword System (working prototype)

The problem: the current filter trusts every keyword equally. One match on الأمن السيبراني (cybersecurity) almost certainly means a cyber document; one match on حصان طروادة (Trojan horse) is usually a political metaphor. The naive fix (require ≥ 2 keywords) throws away all single-hit records — the good ones along with the bad.

The idea: crossing the LLM verdict with each record's keyword-hit count lets us measure every keyword's individual reliability and split the lexicon into tiers by how much “company” a keyword needs before its match can be trusted:

TierMeaningAssignment ruleCount
1Self-sufficient — one hit is enoughappeared alone in a record judged cyber, and its solo records are majority-correct38
2Needs one companionstrongest positive evidence in 2-keyword records64
3Needs two companionsstrongest positive evidence in 3-keyword records34
4Needs heavy companyonly ever seen correct alongside 4+ keywords143

The demotion rule (the heart of the system): a keyword that appears alone in one correctly-judged record does not automatically reach Tier 1 — the majority of its solo records must be correct. حصان طروادة appeared solo in 1 cyber record versus 5 metaphorical ones, so it was demoted. The system also exposed 7 keywords that never produced a single correct match (most notably الباب الخلفي — literal back doors — responsible for 312 corpus records).

How it improves filtering: a tiered acceptance rule — keep a record iff it matches ≥ 1 Tier-1 keyword, or ≥ 2 of Tier ≤ 2, or ≥ 3 of Tier ≤ 3, or ≥ 4 of any tier. This keeps the genuinely good single-hit records while suppressing metaphorical noise — a precision lift without sacrificing recall.

5. The Gap: A Sample Is Not Enough for Production

  • Only 286 of the 1,209 keywords appeared in the judged sample at all — 923 keywords have no evidence and cannot be assigned a tier.
  • Some Tier-1 assignments rest on one or two solo records — statistically fragile.
  • The 24 newly mined keywords (VPN and friends) have not entered any evaluation yet.

The fix: judge the corpus in full. The infrastructure is already built and tested (llm_judge_api.py): the same measured judging protocol, run through the Claude Message Batches API (50% discount), schema-enforced structured output, resumable per chunk. The only missing ingredient is API credit.

7. Expected Deliverables

  1. A fully judged corpus: ~14,260 records, each carrying an LLM verdict + justification + summary — a reusable research asset (classifier training, fine-tuning, follow-up studies).
  2. A complete tier system: every keyword observed in the corpus assigned a tier backed by thousands of verdicts instead of hundreds — rebuilt in seconds at no extra cost.
  3. A second-generation filter: the tiered acceptance rule applied, with expected precision jumping from ~0.68 to ~0.85+ (lenient) while preserving recall — measured, not guessed.
  4. A measured deletion list: keywords proven to be a net liability to the lexicon across the whole corpus.

8. Conclusion

The entire research infrastructure is in place and tested: the filter, the judging protocol, an expanded 1,233-keyword lexicon, and a working prototype of the tier system. What remains is one small budget item — maximum USD 150 — to turn the 300-record sample into full evidence over ~14,260 records, completing an Arabic cybersecurity filtering system whose precision is measured and whose every decision is explainable.
